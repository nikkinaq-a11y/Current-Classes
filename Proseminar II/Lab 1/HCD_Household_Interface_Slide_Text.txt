Slide 1 — Rocker Light Switch

A standard rocker light switch is a strong example of human-centered design because its function is highly discoverable. The raised surface affords pressing, while the two ends of the rocker act as signifiers showing where force should be applied. The switch also uses a familiar natural mapping: pressing one side changes the light to one state, while pressing the opposite side reverses that state. On many switches, “up” corresponds to on and “down” to off, although Norman notes that mappings that feel natural can also depend on cultural convention. The switch provides immediate visual feedback because the room becomes light or dark as soon as it is operated, as well as tactile feedback from the physical movement or click of the switch. Its conceptual model is simple: the switch controls the electrical state of the light. The interface primarily communicates through vision and touch, with sound sometimes providing additional feedback through the click.


Slide 2 — Scissors

Scissors demonstrate several HCD principles working together. The finger holes afford insertion, while their visible shape also acts as a signifier indicating where the user's fingers belong. The sizes of the holes create physical constraints that limit how the scissors can reasonably be held. The interface also has extremely clear mapping: closing the handles closes the blades, while opening the handles opens them. Because the mechanism is physically visible, the user can form an accurate conceptual model of the system without instructions. Movement of the blades provides immediate visual and tactile feedback about the result of the user's action. The scissors also contain anti-affordances: solid portions of the handles prevent fingers from moving into inappropriate locations, while the joined mechanism limits the blades to their intended range of movement. Vision and touch work together to communicate how the object should be used.


Slide 3 — Lever-Style Sink Faucet

A lever-style faucet is effective because its physical form makes the available actions relatively discoverable. The protruding handle affords grasping and moving, while its shape acts as a signifier that communicates that it can be lifted, lowered, or rotated. The faucet typically uses natural mapping: lifting the lever increases water flow, lowering it decreases flow, and moving it toward one side changes the temperature toward hot or cold. Red and blue markings, when present, provide additional signifiers for temperature. The faucet produces strong and immediate feedback through several senses: the user sees the amount of water, hears the flow, and feels both the pressure and temperature. The system therefore creates a clear conceptual model connecting the position of the lever with water flow and temperature. The sink basin also acts as an anti-affordance by containing the water and preventing it from spreading across the surrounding surface. The interface relies on vision, touch, hearing, and temperature sensation.


Slide 4 — Toaster

A traditional toaster provides a clear conceptual model because its major functions are physically visible. The slots afford insertion of appropriately sized food, while their narrow dimensions also operate as constraints, discouraging objects that do not fit. The large side lever affords pushing and serves as a strong signifier for starting the toasting process. A numbered or graduated browning dial communicates that different settings correspond to different levels of browning. The interface also provides clear feedback: the lever stays down while the toaster is operating and then releases with an audible click as the toast visibly pops upward when the process is complete. These behaviors help the user understand the system as a sequence: insert bread → choose intensity → press lever → wait → toast rises. The user's senses of vision, touch, hearing, and smell all provide information about the interface and its state.


Slide 5 — Electrical Outlet and Plug

A household electrical plug and outlet demonstrate HCD largely through affordances and constraints. The openings in the outlet afford insertion, but only for objects with an appropriate shape and orientation. The geometry of the plug and outlet therefore creates a strong physical constraint that limits incorrect actions. In polarized plugs, differently sized prongs further restrict orientation. The shape of the outlet itself acts as a signifier because users can visually match the prongs with the corresponding openings before attempting insertion. The interface has a straightforward conceptual model: inserting a compatible plug establishes an electrical connection that provides power to the device. There is also an important anti-affordance: the surrounding plastic plate blocks access to most of the electrical components inside the wall. Feedback is somewhat indirect—the connected appliance powers on rather than the outlet itself visibly changing state—but the physical resistance and final seated position provide tactile feedback during insertion. Vision and touch are the main senses used.


Slide 6 — Stove Controls That Do Not Match Burner Layout

The intended function of the stove controls is simple: each knob controls one burner and allows the user to adjust its heat. However, many stovetops arrange four burners in a two-by-two spatial layout while placing all four knobs in a straight horizontal row. This creates poor mapping between the controls and the burners. A user must rely on small diagrams or memory to determine which knob corresponds to which burner, making predictable errors such as turning on the wrong burner. The interface could be improved by arranging the controls in the same two-by-two pattern as the burners or positioning each control physically closer to the burner it operates. This would create a natural spatial mapping and reduce the amount of information the user must memorize.


Slide 7 — Unlabeled Multi-Switch Wall Panel

This interface is intended to let a user independently control several lights or fixtures, but identical, unlabeled switches provide almost no information about which switch controls which device. Although the switches themselves clearly afford pressing, the interface lacks useful signifiers and mapping for distinguishing their individual functions. The user often has to rely on trial and error, flipping switches until the desired light responds. The problem becomes worse when the controlled device is in another room or outside because there may be little immediate feedback. The interface could be improved by grouping switches according to location, adding small labels or icons, or arranging them spatially to correspond with the position of the lights they control. Even better, related controls could be placed closer to the objects they operate.


Slide 8 — TV Remote with Too Many Similar Buttons

The remote is intended to provide convenient access to television functions, but its large number of similarly shaped buttons creates poor discoverability. Many controls have weak or ambiguous signifiers, small labels, unfamiliar icons, and little visual hierarchy separating frequently used actions from rarely used ones. Although every button affords pressing, the user may not understand what pressing it will do. This forces users to memorize functions or use trial and error, which contradicts Norman's goal of making important information available through the interface itself. The design could be improved by reducing the number of visible controls, making frequently used functions such as power, volume, navigation, and playback visually and physically distinct, grouping related buttons together, and moving advanced functions into a secondary interface. Better organization would create a clearer conceptual model and reduce cognitive load.


Slide 9 — Dishwasher Controls Hidden on the Top Edge of the Door

The dishwasher interface is intended to allow the user to select a wash cycle, start the machine, and understand its current status. However, controls placed along the top edge of the door become invisible when the dishwasher is closed. This reduces discoverability and can eliminate useful feedback during operation. Once the machine is running, a user may not be able to see the remaining time, cycle stage, or whether the dishwasher successfully started. The system's state therefore becomes difficult to understand without opening the door or waiting for indirect sounds. The design could be improved by placing essential controls and status information on the front surface or by providing a small exterior display or projected indicator showing whether the dishwasher is running, finished, paused, or experiencing an error. This would preserve a clean appearance while still communicating important system information.


Slide 10 — Shower Temperature Control

The shower control is intended to regulate water flow and temperature, but the relationship between handle position and resulting temperature can be difficult to discover. Small or absent hot/cold indicators create weak signifiers, while the rotational movement of the handle may not clearly communicate how much the temperature will change. More importantly, the system often suffers from delayed feedback: after the user moves the handle, water already inside the pipes must flow out before the new temperature reaches the user. This delay can cause the person to overcorrect, producing water that becomes unexpectedly too hot or too cold. The design could be improved with larger and clearer temperature markings, a more direct mapping between handle position and temperature, and ideally a temperature display or thermostatic control that allows the user to select a specific temperature. Better feedback would make the system safer and easier to predict.
